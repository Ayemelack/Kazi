# Part Three — The Serpent of the Mountain-Eye

Now I must tell you, said Cagn, of the oldest serpent. You will find it in every
chronicle, mine and the scribes of the north and the traders of the coast, and
you will find it wearing different skins: in one it is a python, in another a
dragon of the seaweed country, in a third a white rope stretching from the horn
of the moon. But in the Zulu Chronicle — the true one, the one carved in the face
of the Loft — the oldest serpent has only one shape, and it is the shape of the
mountain itself, and its name is uKhahlamba, the Barrier of Spears, and they are
wrong, all of them, who say that the mountains were simply there before us.

The mountains were not there. The mountain was one serpent, curled around the
secret of the world, and the world is that round enchanted object — call it what
you will — the egg, the whale-bone, the bowl of being — that the serpent has
guarded since before the fire was stolen, and the reason the serpent guards it is
not devotion and not duty but that the serpent is lonely, and the secret is the
only thing in all the world that will stay still and let the serpent be the
serpent, coiled about it, sleeping against it, breathing against it for the long
fathomless dark before the first clan lit the first fire and the loneliness of
the snake became, at last, the patience of the world.

I have spoken to the serpent. Not often, and never long, and always at the price
of a little of myself, because to speak to the oldest one is to lean over a well
and see your own face at the bottom wearing another age of your body. But I have
spoken to it, and I will tell you what the serpent told me, and you will write it
down, and when the world ends into the word, as I have promised you it will, this
will be the part that comes back green.

The serpent told me that it had not chosen its work. It had been chosen, the way
rain is chosen to fall and iron is chosen to be patient and the moon is chosen to
hide its face on the three dark nights of shame and delight. When the world was
first struck off the living clay, said the serpent, there was no shape to it, and
no edge, and no name, and a thing with no name and no edge cannot be held, and
cannot be kept, and cannot be guarded, and so the first shapers of the world
summoned the ones who would keep it, and they set the keepers at the edges, and
the keepers at the edges were the great snakes of the four directions, and of
them all only the southern snake held its post down the whole of its unknowable
length, and that is why the mountains of the south are the oldest and the longest
and the loneliest and the most patient of any through all that chain, because the
southern serpent never left, never slept past its sentinel hour, never once asked
who was holding the northern sky while it held the southern.

And then the people came. And the serpent, which had been chosen to hold the
edge of the world, found itself holding the edge of something else entirely, a
thing it had never been told about and never suspected: the edge of *us*. And it
learned, in its patient blood, the terrible old muscles of the earth: that a
guard is only a guard while what it guards is quiet, and that once what it
guards is alive and walking and making its own fire and its own iron and its own
stories, the guarding changes, and the guard changes, and the long watch becomes
a long argument, and the argument becomes a war, and the war becomes a truce, and
the truce becomes a dance, and the dance becomes — as it always does, as it
always must — the slow interlocking of two bodies that cannot decide whether they
are master and slave or mother and child.

The serpent was the edge. The people were the walking. And the place where the
edge and the walking met — that seam, that shimmer, that long bright compromise
of weather and stone and will — is what the chroniclers call the Great Rift of
the nation's birth, and the serpent is in it, and the people are in it, and I
am in it, and you, joy-bringer, are in it even now as you write, though you have
not felt the crack you sit at the mouth of yet.

Let me tell you the story of the seam, the way the serpent told it to me, and
then I will tell you the way the people told it to me, and the two stories are
the same story wearing different skins, and that is the whole trick of keeping
the world whole: to hold the two skins around one truth and never let them tear.

The serpent's story of the seam is this. There came a night, in the age of my
waking that you have already let me tell you, when the first mother who had found
my fire climbed the lower slopes of the long mountain to ask the mountain a
question. There is nothing in the world, said the first mother to the stone, that
does not end, and I have come to ask you, old edge, to make an end for my
children that is a good end, an end that they will walk toward willingly the way
the river walks toward the sea, an end that is not death but only the other side
of the eating, so that when they pass your cold shoulder going down, they will be
passing into something and not out of everything.

And the serpent, which had never been asked a question in all its unthinkable
watch, was surprised, and when a being of the oldest kind is surprised, it does
not answer at once; it uncoils a little of its enormous self and looks, and what
it saw was the first mother standing on the slope in the light of the pale and
remembered fire, and she was the first thing in the whole unthinkable watch that
had ever stood on the slope and asked instead of taken.

So the serpent considered the question, and considered it the way the mountain
considers the weather, which is to say not quickly and not idly and not forget——
fully, and when the serpent had considered, it rose a little way out of its
coil, the way a river rises in its banks in the rains, and it answered the first
mother, and the answer came out of the mountain like water out of a wound:

*I will make you an ending that is a doorway. When your children die, they will
not fall into nothing; they will fall into the talking. They will fall into the
tongues of the ones they leave, and the ones who leave them will shake with the
fall of them, and the shake will be the next scroll of the story, and in that way
the end will be the beginning, and the beginning will be the end, and the long
round will turn, and turn, and turn, and it will be the only turn that does not
run out.*

And the first mother heard, and she understood, and she blessed the mountain, and
she walked back down to the fire, and she told no one what she had asked, because
a woman does not waste a blessing by speaking it at the wrong time and to the
wrong ears. But she remembered. And remembering is a form of guarding, and the
form of guarding that she chose is the reason that the serpent and the people
came, over the long ages, into the interlocking, into the seam, into the one
long argument that grew into the dance.

The people's story of the seam is older to their tongues and younger to their
blood. It tells of the time when the first clans of the first family, walking
southward out of the long grass of the iron's coming, saw the Barrier of Spears
rise up against the sky and despaired, and sent their bravest, a woman named in
the old gloss *the one who looks for the path*, to find a way over or past or
through the wall of stone. And the story says she walked along the foot of the
mountain for nine days, and on the ninth day the mountain made a sound, and she
looked up, and she saw that the mountain had opened an eye — a single eye of
sheer black stone in the grey face of the barrier, an eye that was not a cave and
not a shadow but an actual eye, watchful and patient and terrible and old — and
the eye blinked, and when it blinked, water came out of the mountain, and the
water poured down the flank in a bright cold thread, and the thread became a
brook, and the brook became the path, and the woman who looked for the path
followed the eye's water up and over and beyond the Barrier of Spears, and where
she crossed, all the first clans crossed after her, and where she drank, they
drank, and the brook is still there, and the eye is still there, and if you know
where to look you can see it watching, patient and terrible and old, and it is
still keeping the promise it made to the seam: that the ending would be a doorway,
and the doorway would be water, and the water would be the way through.

You see, said Cagn, that the two stories are the same, and that is the whole of
it. The serpent kept the edge. The people walked the edge. The mother asked for
the turning. The mountain gave her the water. There is no contradiction between
the stone that watches and the water that carries; they are one story in two
skins, and a chronicler who writes only one of the skins is a liar, and a
chronicler who writes both is a keeper, and you, joy-bringer, will write both,
for I have told you both, and the Loft will hold them as it has always held them.

But there is a third thing that the serpent told me, and it is the thing I have
never told anyone, and now, because the Loft is cracking and the letters are
wearing and the world is ending into the word, I will tell it to you, and you
will write it at the place in the Chronicle where it belongs, which is not at the
beginning and not at the end but at the exact centre, because that is where the
secret heart of any kept thing must live, so that all the other letters lean on
it and are kept whole by the weight of their own leaning.

The serpent told me that the object it guards — the egg, the whale-bone, the bowl
of being — is not inside the mountain. It is the mountain. The mountain is the
coil. The coil is the guard. And the guard is everything, and everything is the
guard, and the reason the serpent cannot leave its post is that its post is its
own body, which means the serpent cannot be saved and cannot be freed and cannot
be released from its watch except by becoming something other than itself, and
becoming something other than yourself is the one transformation that the
patient ones cannot make, because it requires an act of forgetting, and the
patient ones are made entirely of remembering.

Which, said Cagn, and he smiled the smile that the chronicler came to fear and
then to long for, is why it takes a trickster to end the world and begin it
again. The patient ones guard forever. The trickster forgets on purpose. The
world turns on nothing grander than that.

And he leaned forward, and his great old eyes held the pale failing sun, and he
said:

Now write that down. Write it in the centre. And when you have written it, I will
tell you of Shaka.
