# Part One — The Awakening

This is the chronicle they swore would never be told, and it begins where all true
things begin: not at the beginning, but at the ending, for a story is only a road
you walk in order to look back at where you have been.

The cliff called Loft stood at the lip of the world, and it stood alone. It was a
shelf of white stone, older than the rivers, older than the names of the rivers,
and the herdsmen who slept beneath it in the season of dust swore that on certain
nights the stone could be heard to breathe. They said it breathed in the manner of
a sleeper who dreams of standing, a sleeper who has not moved for a thousand
summers and knows that on the morning he opens his eyes the sun will have a
different hunger in its heart. The herdsmen were not wrong. They were merely
early.

The one who was neither herdsman nor warrior, neither child nor elder, but that
clumsy in-between thing called a chronicler, stood at the foot of the cliff and
watched the world end. His name was Lonwabo, which means he who has joy, and it
was a joke that his grandmother had played on him, because he had been born under
a roof of grey cloud and had wept at everything he had seen since. He carried the
ink-horn of a woman who had died before he was born, her petition to be remembered,
and his paper was the stripped bark of the fever tree, and his task, given to him
by the only king who had ever trusted him, was to write down whatever happened
next so that whatever came after would know the shape of what had been.

The world was ending in the way that a song ends: not all at once, but by
agreement. The sea, which had always thrown itself at the land in rage, had begun
to fall back from the shore as though it had remembered an appointment and was
sorry to be late. The fish lay on the mud flats gasping, and the gulls screamed
over them in a language that had too many words for hunger. The sun, too, had
changed. It rose pale and rose late, and when it crossed noon it did not burn the
shade but only stood there, watchful, like a bull that has heard something move in
the long grass and is deciding, very slowly, whether to charge.

And there was the sound. Lomwabo heard it first as a drumming under the soles of
his bare feet, the kind of drumming that a great forge makes when the hammers of
many men fall together at the same heartbeat. But there were no forges within a
day's walk of Loft, and the sound grew, and it climbed his legs into his chest,
and he understood then that the sound was not coming toward him. It was coming
from inside the cliff. It was the breathing of the sleeper, and the sleeper was
waking.

A crack ran up the face of the white stone. It was not a wild crack. It was
patient and deliberate, a crack that had been waiting in the rock for a very long
time, like the seam of a pod that only bursts when the seed inside is ready.
Lonwabo watched the crack widen, and he watched a fist of dark stone push through,
and he watched the stone crumble, and then he watched the hand that had made it.

It was the hand of a creature that was old in a way that had nothing to do with
years. It was thin and long-fingered and the colour of honey that has been kept
too long in the dark, and at the wrist it narrowed to a kind of elbow that bent
the wrong way, so that the creature could have scratched the back of its own neck
from its own chest or reached behind itself for a sword that belonged to someone
else. The hand gripped the edge of the crack, and the crack widened again, and a
head emerged.

The head, too, was the head of a creature that had once been something else, or
would one day be something else, and had paused, for the length of the world's
middle age, in the shape that means *trickster*. It was narrow and heart-shaped
and the eyes were impossibly large, and they were the colour of the sky when the
sky has decided something terrible and is keeping it to itself. They swivelled on
the stalk of a face that could not stop moving, and they found Lonwabo, and
whatever they saw there made the creature laugh, or cough, or sneeze, or pray — it
was hard to tell, because with creatures like this it is hard to tell whether the
noise they make when they see you is delight or mourning.

"Cagn," said the creature, and the word fell out of its mouth and lay on the
ground between them like a stone. "Long will I sleep. Longer will I be forgotten.
Longest of all will it take me to wake." It stretched, and the crack in the cliff
widened further, and rock fell, and the creature stepped out of the mountain the
way a man steps out of a doorway, and looked up at the pale sun, and nodded, once,
as though the sun had kept an appointment it had almost doubted.

"You have been writing, chronicler," said Cagn. "Show me."

Lonwabo had not moved. He could not have moved if the cliff had fallen on him,
because his legs had made an agreement with the ground not to take him anywhere
until they had understood what was standing in front of him. But his hands were
older than his legs, and they had their own opinions, and they lifted the fever-
tree bark and held it out, and Cagn took it, and read.

He read slowly. He read the way a very old man reads a letter from a friend he
believed dead, turning his face slightly away from the words as though the words
were light and his eyes were no longer accustomed to brightness. And when he was
finished, he did what no one — not the king who had ordered the chronicle, not the
grandmother who had laughed at his name, not the sky itself — had ever done.

He folded the bark carefully, the way you fold a good thing that you mean to keep,
and he put it inside the leather of his own chest, and when the bark touched his
skin it glowed faintly, as though it had been waiting a very long time for exactly
this touch.

"Hear me, joy-bringer," said Cagn, and now his voice was not the voice of a
trickster at all. It was the deep, patient, unmovable voice of the mountain that
had just released him. "The world is ending, but you have it backwards. The world
does not end by breaking. The world ends by forgetting. Everything that we do not
remember does not happen again. Every story that we fail to keep airtight, every
name we let fall through the crack in the floor of the year, the world loses that
thing, and when the world has lost enough things, it will have lost the shape of
itself, and then it will fold up like an old woman's shell and go back to the
water."

He turned and looked at the cliff, and Lonwabo saw that the crack was not the only
mark on the face of the stone. There were other marks, older marks, marks that
were not cracks at all but writing. Someone had carved the entire cliff with a
chronicle in letters too large for a man to read, and the letters were wearing
away, and where a letter had worn away, the stone beneath it was soft and grey,
like bone that has been sucked hollow by time.

"That is the Zulu Chronicle," said Cagn, "and I am its last keeper. I have slept
in the cliff keeping the letters whole with my dreaming, and now the dream has
broken, so the letters are breaking, and when the letters are gone the things they
name will be gone, and when the things they name are gone the world will end in
earnest, with no one left to notice. You, joy-bringer, will write them down faster
than they wear away. That is your task. That is why you were born under the grey
cloud. That is why your grandmother gave you the name that makes you weep."

Lonwabo looked at the cliff, at the enormous fading letters, and he felt the ink
in his horn grow heavy with the fright of the work.

"I am only a man," he said. "These letters are taller than mead-halls. I will die
before I write the first word."

"No," said Cagn. "You will not. While you write the Chronicle, the Chronicle will
write you. That is the secret of the old masters of Wotanism, and it is the only
secret worth keeping. When you carry a true story, the story does not travel in
you; you travel in it. Time will stop bending you. The rope of your life will hang
loose in the world's hands. You will be as old as the letters you keep, no older,
no younger, and you will be just as long. Sit. Sit at my feet, joy-bringer, and
open your horn, for I am going to tell you the whole of it, from the dawn of the
first clan to the morning that cracks in the sky above us now, and you are going
to write it all down, and the world is going to end, and then — and only then —
it is going to begin again, because that is the thing about a chronicle: it is
the only door in the world that opens both ways."

And the creature that the herdsmen had called the mountain's breath, the creature
that in the oldest tongue of the oldest people was called Cagn, the Mantis, the
Old Master, the Cracker of the Loft, sat down at the foot of the cliff in the
light of the pale and failing sun, and it began to speak.
