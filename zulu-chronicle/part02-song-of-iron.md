# Part Two — The Song of Iron

The first thing the old master taught the chronicler — before the first clan, before
the first spear, before the name of the first king had ever been a word that a
woman could say without lifting her chin — was that the world is not made of things.
The world is made of patience. Everything that is hard, said Cagn, was once soft and
is only pretending otherwise. A mountain is a slow river. An iron spearhead is a
long thought. A man is a story that has agreed to stand up and bear the weather for
a generation, and the weather is nothing but the world telling the story its
continuation, and the continuation is always, always, always the same three words:
*not yet, not yet, not yet.*

"When the world was young," said Cagn, "younger than the Loft, younger than the sea,
younger even than the moon's boredom, there were no clans and no iron and no kings,
and there was only one fire. It burned at the centre of the earth, in a cave that
had no name, because nothing had ever needed to find it. I kept that fire. It was
my first task and my first joy. I fed it with sticks that I broke myself, and I
watched it the way a father watches the sleeping face of a newborn thing, and I
believed that I was its keeper.

"But I was young then, in the way that I have never been young since. And when a
creature is young, it does something that the old ones never do: it leaves what it
is guarding to go and look at something else that it has no business with. I left
the fire to climb a hill, because there was a sound coming from the other side of
the hill, and I wanted to know what the sound was. It was a good sound. It was the
sound of the first woman laughing at the first joke, and the joke was about the
moon, and the moon heard it, and the moon blushed, and that is how the moon got its
three days of darkness in the month — the only secret about the moon that I have
ever told a man, and now I have told you, and you will write it, and it will be
true because you have written it."

Cagn paused, and looked at the sky, and it seemed to Langyo — for that is how the
name is written in the Chronicle, though the tongue said Lonwabo — that the pale
sun flickered, as though it had overheard and was offended, or amused, or both.

While he was gone, said Cagn, the first woman found the fire. She did not steal
it. She was not a thief. She was a mother, and a mother has no time to distinguish
between what is given and what is found, because to a mother everything found is
gift and everything gift is found. She took a burning stick home to her child,
who was cold. The child was the first child, and being the first, it had no name,
because no one had yet discovered that a thing without a name is a thing that can
be lost and never asked after. Because the child was cold and unnamed, the mother
named it Fire, and fed it, and the child Fire grew, and grew warm, and grew hot,
and grew into the fire that every hearth of every clan has kept burning ever
since, and there is not a hearth fire in all the world that does not trace its
blood back to that one stolen, borrowed, gifted, found stick of the first mother.

Now — and here Cagn's voice changed, and became the voice he used when he was
about to tell the part that mattered — there are two ways of keeping a fire. The
first way is to guard it. The second way is to teach it. I had chosen the first
way, and I lost it. The mother chose the second way, and she multiplied it past
my counting. That is the lesson of the fire, joy-bringer, and when you understand
it you will understand the Zulu Chronicle from the first letter to the last: the
only things that live are the things we give away on purpose, and the only things
that die are the things we hold so tightly that they cannot breathe.

The fire spread, and with the fire came the clans. The first clans were not born
grown. They were born as families are born, one and then another and then
another, each one a small knot of humans standing in the long grass in the light
of their own hearth, learning to look at each other and say, without words, *we
are the ones who sit on this side of the fire and the world that burns is on the
other.* For a long age that was all the nation there was: fire, and the family,
and the long grass, and the wind that moved the grass like the hand of a god
checking that everything was still where it ought to be.

But the wind was not checking. The wind was trying to tell them something, and it
took them a long age to hear it, because the wind was many-tongued, and spoke in
the grass, and the grass was always telling them the same thing in a language that
sounded like weather.

*Look up,* said the grass. *Look up, look up, there is something coming that is
hungrier than you, and it walks on two legs, and it has fire of its own.*

The thing that was coming walked on two legs, and it had fire of its own, and its
name — I will write it once and then we will both pretend I did not — was Bantu,
which is to say *the People*, and yes, joy-bringer, I see that you have flinched,
and yes, they wore our skins and walked in our wake, and yes, it is true that the
ancestors of the whole far nation of Zulu came down out of the north in a long
migration of fire and iron and hunger, and yes, they were the same clay as the
first family, and that is the whole thought: there is no enemy clay. There is only
the one clay, and the one fire, and the long coming.

The first clans of the first family did not fight the coming thing. That is the
only mercy of that age, and I will not make a lie of it for poetry: they did not
fight, because they did not yet have the skill to fight, which is the same as
saying they did not yet have the hate to fight, because no one can fight well who
has not learned, at some long table, the art of remembering a wrong. The first
family was poor in wrongs. They had the fire, and the wind, and the long grass,
and the moon that hid its face for three nights every month out of shame or
delight, and they did not know that a people can be rich in wrongs, and that that
kind of wealth is the heaviest of all carries.

And so the first family moved, and the coming people walked into the land of the
long grass, and the first family did not stand in their way, because the way was
very long and there was, after all, enough grass for everyone, or nearly, or so it
seemed in that gentle age when the sun still knew its own heart.

The coming people brought with them an iron thing. It was not a spear. It was a
song. It was the song of iron, the long green ache of the smith, the *nkalimeva,
nkalimeva*, the iron sings, the iron sings back, the iron remembers the mountain
it came from and the fire that first woke it and the hand that shaped it and the
grip that made it a tool or a killing thing or both, for iron is patient, and
iron is the patience of the mountain made walkable, and iron does not care which
of the two it becomes.

I heard the song of iron before I saw its maker, said Cagn. I lay in the long
grass with my cow-shoulder blade in my hand — for in that age I wore the clever
bones of the country as I do now, though I was less old then — and I listened to
the smith. I listened to him for a night and a day, and by the end of the second
night I had learned, from the rhythm of his hammer alone, that the iron was
coming out of his fire in the shape of a spear.

And I was afraid. I, Cagn, the old master, the keeper of the one fire, was afraid
of the patience of iron. I have never told this to any creature; I tell it to you
now because you are the chronicle and the chronicle does not flinch. I was afraid
because I understood that the shape of the world had just changed tail, and I was
on the wrong side of the change. Everything that I had guarded for the long age —
the fire, the grass, the wind, the moon's shyness — was going to be hammered now,
patiently, into a killing shape, and there was nothing in the grass that could
withstand the patience of iron, and there was nothing in me that could, either,
because I am only the keeper of fire, and iron is the keeper of the mountain, and
the mountain is older than the fire and will be older than the fire's ending.

And so I did the only thing that the old master could do. I did not fight the
coming iron. I taught it. I went to the smith, and I sat at his fire, and I told
him the one thing about iron that kept me alive through the long age of spears:

*Iron is patient, but a story is more patient. If you make your spear of mountain,
I will make my spear of remembering, and when your iron has rusted to red dust in
the rain of ten thousand years, the remembering will still be sharp, and it will
still be cutting the same wrong, and it will still be thirsty.*

The smith did not understand me. He was the first smith, and no one has ever
understood me the first time. But he was a good man, as smiths are, and he did
what good men do with words they do not understand: he remembered them, and he
carried them, and when his grandson's grandson in the eighth generation stood
before his own red forge with the iron glowing and heard in his own heart, as if
remembered, the words *the remembering will still be sharp*, he understood that
the stranger who had sat at his grandfather's fire had been telling the truth all
along, and he hammered his spear into the shape of a thing that could hold a
story, and that is how the first song-blade was made, and how the first praise-
name was carried, and how the Zulu Chronicle first began to be possible.

For the Chronicle is not the history of the spears, joy-bringer. The spears are
little things, and they break, and they are melted down into plough blades and
back again into spears, and none of it matters. The Chronicle is the history of
the patience of iron learning the patience of the word, the long hammering of the
green ache into the shape of remembering, the slow work of a hundred generations
sharpening the same story on the mountain of the mind until it holds an edge that
rust cannot reach.

And when the first song-blade was found, the clans that had watched the coming
of the iron learned something about themselves that they had never known: that
they too were iron. That they too were patience. That between the fire that the
first mother stole and the mountain that remembers, there lay a long bright chord
of becoming, and that the name of that chord is *Nasuni* — the People of the
Spear — and that everything that followed, every king, every battle, every
marriage, every murder, every mercy of the long vivid history of the Zulu, is
only the hammering of that one chord into a shape that the world, when it
forgets everything else, will still be able to sing.

The first smith set the first song-blade into the hand of the first king, and
the first king raised it above the long grass, and the wind moved through the
grass, and in the grass the voice of the first family said its ancient three
words:

*Not yet, not yet, not yet.*

But it was time. And we — you and I, joy-bringer, the keeper and the chronicler,
sitting at the foot of the cracking Loft with the pale sun dying over our
shoulders — we are here to write down exactly how time began.
